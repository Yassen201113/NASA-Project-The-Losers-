01

Who am I?

I am Curiosity, a robotic rover built by NASA to explore Mars. I launched from Earth on November 26, 2011, and landed inside Gale Crater on August 6, 2012. My body is about the size of a small car, but I am a mobile science laboratory. I have six wheels, cameras, a robotic arm, a drill, and instruments that examine rocks, soil, and the atmosphere. I do not travel quickly, because every movement must be planned around the rough Martian ground. Before I move, engineers study images and choose a safe route. My landing was a major engineering challenge: a special system slowed me down and lowered me gently to the surface. Since then, I have been sending photographs and measurements that help scientists understand how Mars changed over billions of years.

02

Why was I sent?

The main question behind my mission is whether ancient Mars ever had environments that could support microbial life. Scientists are not expecting me to find living organisms directly. Instead, I search for clues in rocks and minerals: evidence of water, useful chemical ingredients, and conditions that may once have been suitable for life. Gale Crater was selected because it contains a tall layered mountain, Mount Sharp, and signs that water shaped the region long ago. My instruments can study the chemistry of a rock, identify minerals, and examine tiny samples after drilling. My robotic arm can place tools close to a target, while cameras help the team understand the landscape. Each measurement is one piece of a much larger investigation into the planet's past.

03

What did I discover?

My work revealed that Gale Crater once contained ancient environments with liquid water. I studied sedimentary rocks that formed in places where water gathered, including evidence of an old lake. Some of the rocks contain clay minerals, which can form when water interacts with other materials. I also detected organic molecules, carbon-based compounds that are important ingredients for life. These findings do not prove that life ever existed on Mars. Organic molecules can form through nonliving processes, too. But the results show that ancient Mars had water and chemistry that make it an important place to study. By comparing different layers of rock, scientists can reconstruct changes in climate and environment, much like reading pages in a history book written by nature.

04

Where am I now?

I continue exploring the lower slopes and layers of Mount Sharp. As I climb and examine new rocks, I move through chapters of Martian history preserved in the landscape. Different layers formed under different conditions, so their minerals can reveal whether the environment became wetter, drier, or more chemically challenging over time. My cameras take detailed images, and my instruments measure the composition of targets selected by scientists. The work is slow and careful. A wheel must avoid dangerous rocks, a drill must be used thoughtfully, and every command must account for the delay in communication between Earth and Mars. Dust, cold temperatures, and the age of my equipment also make the mission challenging. Yet each successful measurement adds another clue to the story of this world.

05

A message from me

I am not looking for a simple yes-or-no answer. My mission studies habitability: whether a place has the ingredients and conditions life could use. Earth and Mars formed in the same Solar System, yet their histories became very different. Understanding that change teaches scientists about water, climate, and life's limits. My discoveries help future explorers choose where to investigate and which instruments to send. I am a robot, but my work depends on engineers, programmers, geologists, chemists, and patient teamwork. Every image and measurement is checked against evidence. That is how curiosity becomes reliable science.
