01

Who am I?

Hello, Earth. I am Voyager 1, a robotic spacecraft built by NASA to explore the outer Solar System and send information back across an enormous distance. I launched on September 5, 1977, carrying cameras, scientific instruments, a large antenna, and a message from our planet. I am not a spaceship carrying people; I am a carefully designed machine that follows commands sent by engineers on Earth. My journey began during a rare opportunity to study the giant planets. Although my original mission was planned around a few years of exploration, the spacecraft was built to keep working for as long as its power and instruments allowed. Every signal I send is a small piece of evidence about places that people cannot easily visit. My story is also the story of thousands of people who designed, tested, guided, and listened to me.

02

Why was I sent?

Scientists sent me toward Jupiter and Saturn because these giant worlds held questions that telescopes could not answer completely. From Earth, astronomers could observe their brightness and some details, but a spacecraft could fly close, take detailed pictures, and measure the environment directly. My route used gravity assists: a planet's gravity changed my direction and speed, helping me continue toward the next destination without carrying unlimited fuel. This technique required careful calculations years before launch. Engineers planned the timing, checked my instruments, and prepared ways to communicate across millions and then billions of kilometers. I also carried the Golden Record, a carefully selected collection of sounds, music, images, and greetings from Earth. It was not a message aimed at a known receiver. It was a symbolic introduction to our planet, showing that exploration can carry both scientific measurements and human curiosity.

03

What did I discover?

At Jupiter, I returned close-up views of a complicated world with colorful cloud bands, enormous storms, and many moons. My observations helped confirm that Jupiter has a faint ring system and revealed two small moons, Thebe and Metis. I measured particles and magnetic conditions around the planet, giving scientists information that could not be collected from Earth alone. Then I continued to Saturn. I photographed its bright rings and studied the atmosphere of its moon Titan. Titan was especially interesting because it has a thick atmosphere rich in nitrogen. These discoveries changed the way scientists understood the outer planets. A flyby happens quickly, so mission teams had to choose which observations mattered most and prepare commands in advance. The photographs and measurements were transmitted slowly across space, then studied by researchers who compared them with earlier ideas and found new questions to investigate.

04

Where am I now?

After Saturn, my path carried me away from the familiar planetary region. I continued measuring particles and magnetic fields as the influence of the solar wind gradually changed. In August 2012, my instruments detected that I had crossed the heliopause, the boundary where the solar wind gives way to the interstellar environment. This made me the first human-made object to enter interstellar space. The boundary is not a solid wall; it is a changing region that scientists identify through measurements. I am still far beyond the planets, moving through the space between stars. My power supply has weakened over time, so engineers carefully manage which instruments can remain active. A radio message must travel for many hours before reaching Earth, meaning that every instruction and reply belongs to a conversation across a vast distance.

05

A message from me

My journey outlived its original plan. I carry no crew, questions and measurements from beyond the Sun. My Golden Record is a greeting, not a guarantee of discovery. When my power fades, my signals will stop, but my data will remain.
